<p>The first question that reaches us on WhatsApp is almost always the same one: what size rug fits my living room. The answer depends less on the size of the room than you would expect, and more on how the furniture sits.</p>

<h2>The common mistake: buying too small</h2>

<p>Most people buy a rug two sizes smaller than they need. The reasoning makes sense — a larger rug costs more, and the dimensions sound big written down. But a rug that is too small makes a room look smaller, not larger. It floats in the middle of the floor with no relationship to the furniture, and the eye reads it as a disconnected island.</p>

<p>The simple rule: the rug should touch the furniture. If no sofa leg stands on it, it is too small.</p>

<h2>Three possible layouts</h2>

<h3>1. All legs on the rug</h3>

<p>The most generous layout. Every sofa and armchair sits fully on the rug, with at least 20 cm of margin around them. This creates a room-within-a-room effect and works well in large living rooms or open-plan spaces where you need to draw a line between the seating area and the dining table.</p>

<p>You need: 200×300 or larger. In a standard Israeli apartment this is almost always too big.</p>

<h3>2. Front legs only</h3>

<p>The most common and most practical layout, and the one we recommend in most cases. The sofa stands with its two front legs on the rug and the back legs off it. The rug connects the sofa to the coffee table and defines the seating area without demanding an enormous size.</p>

<p>You need: 160×230 for an average living room, or 200×290 for a wide one.</p>

<h3>3. A small central rug</h3>

<p>Under the coffee table only, touching no furniture. This is the layout that goes wrong most often. It works only in a genuinely small space — the living room of a two-room apartment, say, where any other option would choke the room.</p>

<p>You need: 120×170.</p>

<h2>How to measure before you buy</h2>

<p>Do not estimate. Take masking tape and mark the rectangle of the size you are considering on the floor. Leave it for a day. Walk through the room, sit on the sofa, see how it feels against the furniture. It is a five-minute check that saves a return.</p>

<p>Check the distance to the walls too. A margin of 20 to 40 cm between rug and wall looks deliberate. Under 15 cm looks like a measuring error, and over 60 cm disconnects the rug from the room.</p>

<h2>One more thing: direction</h2>

<p>A rectangular rug should follow the long axis of the room, not fight it. In a long, narrow living room, a rug laid across the width visually shortens the space. It sounds minor and matters more than you would think.</p>

<h2>And the material</h2>

<p>In Israel it is worth favouring cotton or natural fibres over thick synthetics. In strong direct light, synthetic rugs shine and read as cheap, and they trap heat. A flat cotton rug breathes, cleans easily and ages well.</p>

<p>With children or a pet, a flat weave always beats a long pile: it does not absorb stains deep into the fibre, and vacuuming actually gets everything out.</p>

<h2>Short version</h2>

<p>In an average Israeli living room, 160×230 with the sofa's front legs on the rug is the choice that goes wrong least often. If you are torn between two sizes, take the larger one.</p>
